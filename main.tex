\begin{abstract}
Computer-vision wood identification models can be accurate under controlled
acquisition but unreliable when acquisition changes. MRD-Wood is an empirical
diagnostic protocol for frozen pretrained representations, not a new classifier
or drift detector. Across seven backbones and nine public dataset sources, we
evaluate controlled perturbations, external-source generalization, real
cross-source transfer, and cross-magnification transfer. After controlling for
dataset, backbone, perturbation family, and severity, feature drift retains a
modest association with accuracy drop (partial $r=0.623$,
$\Delta R^2=0.031$); a smaller component transfers across feature spaces
($r=0.178$). The pooled same-space correlation ($r=0.908$) is therefore treated
as an upper bound shaped by severity and same-space coupling. A source probe
further shows that frozen features encode acquisition source more reliably than
cross-source species identity. The domain-specific consequences are severe:
shared-species transfer nearly collapses for
BFS46$\leftrightarrow$FSDM41 (mean accuracy 0.008--0.013), and VN26 exhibits
asymmetric, non-monotone transfer across magnifications, with x50 as the main
failure locus. A reference-bank RBF-MMD monitor reaches batch-level ROC-AUC
0.968 and F1 0.909 over 4,256 synthetic-shift monitoring records and flags
60/70 real-shift batches without
target labels. However, its severity ordering remains non-monotone under shared
kernel bandwidths and is sensitive to reference-bank class composition, so it
is an acquisition-mismatch alarm rather than a calibrated accuracy-loss
estimator. These results locate part of the
laboratory-to-field gap in source-sensitive representation geometry and motivate
species-conditional source invariance across acquisition protocols and optical
scales.
\end{abstract}

\begin{highlights}
\item Multi-dataset benchmark tests wood recognition under acquisition shifts.
\item Controlled drift retains a modest signal beyond shift severity.
\item Frozen features encode acquisition source more reliably than species.
\item Cross-source transfer collapses and magnification transfer is asymmetric.
\item Reference-bank alarms detect acquisition mismatch without target labels.

\section{Introduction}

Computer-vision wood identification (WoodID) is a practical pattern-recognition problem with direct implications for timber legality verification, forest-product quality control, biodiversity protection, and forensic wood inspection. In operational settings, a reliable screening system should help non-specialist users flag suspicious timber and support expert confirmation. Macroscopic computer-vision systems are attractive for this purpose because they can use portable cameras or magnifying devices and avoid the slower preparation required by microscopic analysis. Recent CNN, transformer, and patch-voting methods have therefore achieved strong clean-domain accuracy on controlled wood-image benchmarks \citep{paulafilho2014forest,sun2021smallwood,fabijanska2021woodcore,herrera2026goimai}. Field-oriented WoodID systems such as XyloTron further demonstrate why deployment reliability matters for timber screening and enforcement contexts \citep{ravindran2021field}.

However, deployment reliability is not determined by clean accuracy alone. The same wood species may be imaged under different focus quality, illumination, compression settings, sensor responses, magnifications, or specimen-preparation conditions. These changes preserve the semantic label but alter the input distribution. In WoodID, such distribution shifts are not hypothetical: recent evidence shows that digital perturbations can substantially degrade macroscopic WoodID models \citep{owens2025robustness}. This creates a gap between laboratory evaluation and field use. A model that appears accurate under a fixed acquisition protocol may fail when the acquisition protocol drifts.

Most existing WoodID studies answer the question ``which classifier is accurate?'' rather than ``why does a representation fail?'' A drop in accuracy is an endpoint; it does not reveal whether shifted samples move away from their clean feature locations, whether class geometry collapses, whether spatial representations become unstable, or whether model attention moves to different regions. This distinction is important for two reasons. First, representation-level diagnosis can identify failure mechanisms that are invisible in accuracy tables. Second, an operational system often lacks target-domain labels, so it needs label-free signals that warn when incoming features have drifted away from a trusted clean reference distribution.

This paper addresses these issues through \textbf{MRD-Wood}
(\textbf{M}apping \textbf{R}epresentation \textbf{D}rift for Wood
Recognition), an empirical diagnosis and monitoring protocol rather than a new
classifier or detector. The argument follows a lab-to-field chain. First, an
acquisition gap exists: clean accuracy does not determine robustness. Second,
the gap is measurable through representation displacement. Third, source-vs-
species and cross-space tests show that part of the gap lies in the frozen
representation, not only in visible image degradation. This produces the
paper's two central WoodID-specific findings: a boundary where shared-species
features largely stop transferring across acquisition sources, and an asymmetric
cross-magnification failure centred on the finest optical scale. A clean
reference-bank monitor is then derived as a practical label-free detector of
acquisition mismatch. It detects risk but does not remove the underlying gap or
provide a universal severity scale.

The study is organized around three research questions:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{RQ1:} What component of representation drift remains associated
  with WoodID degradation after accounting for dataset, backbone, perturbation
  family, severity, and same-space coupling?
  \item \textbf{RQ2:} How does source-sensitive representation geometry manifest
  under real cross-source and cross-magnification transfer?
  \item \textbf{RQ3:} To what extent can standard reference-bank statistics turn
  this diagnosis into a label-free acquisition-mismatch alarm?
\end{itemize}

MRD-Wood combines frozen backbones, cosine-kNN decisions, and established
OOD/two-sample statistics in a domain-specific empirical analysis. It
locates part of the lab-to-field gap in frozen feature geometry and then exposes
its two strongest consequences under real acquisition change.

The main contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A controlled, class-conditional diagnosis of the lab-to-field
  gap.} Paired, grouped, and cross-space analyses separate severity-linked,
  same-space, and transferable drift components. The source-versus-species
  probe, below-chance cross-source transfer, and kernel decomposition further
  show that marginal acquisition structure can persist while
  species-conditional geometry is misaligned.
  \item \textbf{A real cross-source transfer boundary.} Shared-species
  recognition nearly collapses across BFS46 and FSDM41 even though acquisition
  source remains readily recoverable from the frozen features.
  \item \textbf{A WoodID-specific cross-magnification asymmetry.} VN26 reveals
  sign-reversing, non-monotone optical-scale transfer with x50 as the locus of
  cross-scale incompatibility.
  \item \textbf{A derived label-free detection protocol.} Standard
  reference-bank statistics detect acquisition mismatch without target labels,
  with explicit limits under unseen shift families and severity ranking.
\end{enumerate}

The margin analysis provides a mechanistic account of how displacement
interacts with decision geometry.

The diagnosis points beyond detection: genuine mitigation will require learning
representations that align species geometry across acquisition sources and
optical scales. The monitor provides an early-warning mechanism, while the two
real-shift findings identify the target of a future species-conditional
source-invariant objective.
\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Wood species recognition and acquisition sensitivity}

Computer-vision wood identification has developed along two lines. Hand-crafted
macroscopic texture and anatomical descriptors with classifier fusion established
that wood texture carries strong species-discriminative cues
\citep{paulafilho2014forest,souza2020brazilian}. Deep transfer learning, patch
voting, and field-oriented acquisition subsequently improved performance on
small, core-image, and less extensively prepared collections
\citep{sun2021smallwood,fabijanska2021woodcore,degeus2021amazon,ravindran2021field}.
Public resources including tropical macroscopic collections, WOOD-AUTH, GOIMAI,
and VN26 further show that recognition can be highly accurate under controlled
acquisition while remaining sensitive to surface preparation, anatomical
section, optical scale, and image resolution
\citep{canosaenz2022tropical,barmpoutis2018woodauth,herrera2026goimai,nguyentrong2023magnification}.

These studies establish that wood images are discriminative. The same literature,
however, cautions that controlled image-level performance can overstate field
utility when data hygiene, specimen-disjoint evaluation, and acquisition
conditions are not handled carefully
\citep{hermanson2011review,ravindran2022caveat}. This is the premise of the
present study: high laboratory accuracy does not establish reliability when the
acquisition protocol drifts. Representative quantitative context is retained in
Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:related_quant}; values are not directly comparable
because datasets and protocols differ.

\subsection{Robustness and deployment shift}

Distribution shift is central to pattern recognition because the test
distribution can change while labels remain fixed. ImageNet-C showed that blur,
noise, compression, and digital distortion can break otherwise accurate networks
\citep{hendrycks2019corruptions}. Domain-adversarial learning and CORAL address
shift from the training side \citep{ganin2016domain,sun2016coral}, while
DomainBed showed that generalization claims are protocol-sensitive
\citep{gulrajani2021domainbed}. Those methods assume multiple domains or target
data during training. Our setting is different: frozen backbones are diagnosed
when target labels and target-domain training data are unavailable.

In WoodID, \citet{owens2025robustness} is the closest robustness anchor. They
used red/blue shifts as sensor-drift proxies, resizing as magnification or
calibration mismatch, scratches as preparation artifacts, and blur as
out-of-focus acquisition. Their model was comparatively robust to scratches and
some red shifts but vulnerable to resizing, blue shift, and larger blur regions.
We build on this deployment perspective without treating our channel result as a
replication or refutation. The discrepancy---blue shift is nearly harmless
here---is plausibly explained by transform definition, frozen rather than
task-trained features, decision rule, and acquisition distribution
(Section~\ref{sec:perturbation_results}). The open questions are whether the
representation itself remains stable and whether failure can be detected without
target labels.

\subsection{Representation analysis, monitoring, and positioning}

Accuracy reports only the final error; representation geometry can reveal why a
decision changes. Feature spaces can be compared across architectures
\citep{kornblith2019representations}, and classification stability depends on
class geometry: a label-preserving perturbation can move a sample toward another
class centroid or outside the clean manifold, turning continuous displacement
into a discrete error. This is the conceptual basis for asking whether the
lab-to-field gap lies partly in the frozen representation. WoodID's spatial
evidence also motivates patch processing and cautious explanations
\citep{selvaraju2020gradcam,bai2021explainable}; extended spatial/CAM-proxy
diagnostics are reported in the Supplementary Material.

OOD methods compare incoming representations with clean references using
confidence, nearest-neighbour or centroid distance, Mahalanobis geometry, and
kernel two-sample tests
\citep{hendrycks2017baseline,lee2018mahalanobis,sun2022knn,gretton2012mmd}.
Label-free monitoring of representation drift against a reference window is
also an established direction in recent ML systems work: ALERT monitors learned
embedding shift rather than raw or hand-engineered features
\citep{tziouvaras2025alert}, DriftLens detects drift from deep representations
for unstructured data streams \citep{greco2024driftlens}, D3M targets
post-deployment failure detection without labels or the base model's training
data \citep{nguyen2025d3m}, and risk monitors for test-time adaptation track
unlabeled performance degradation under growing shift
\citep{schirmer2025monitoring}. Together, these studies establish embedding
divergence as a general monitoring signal for performance degradation.
Recent WoodID work on hyperspectral, in-situ, and high-resolution patch-voting
systems does not address acquisition-shift representation drift, shared-species
cross-source transfer, or cross-magnification asymmetry. MRD-Wood is positioned
accordingly. Its contributions are a controlled decomposition locating part of
the gap in frozen representation geometry, the VN26 cross-magnification
asymmetry, and the real cross-source boundary. The monitor instantiates
established distance and two-sample methods and evaluates their validity under
WoodID acquisition shifts. Comparative detector benchmarking remains outside
this domain-diagnostic scope.

\section{MRD-Wood: Diagnosing Source-Sensitive Representation Geometry}

MRD-Wood follows a diagnostic chain. Acquisition change perturbs a frozen
representation; controlled analyses separate
the severity-linked, same-space, and transferable components of that change; and
real cross-source and cross-magnification tests expose where species geometry no
longer transfers. Figure~\ref{fig:mrd_framework} summarizes this logic. A
reference-bank mismatch alarm is derived only after these boundaries are
established. Spatial token clusters and CAM-proxy redistribution are retained as
secondary views of internal organization, not as primary evidence.

\begin{figure}[pos=htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{results/figures/mrd_wood_overview_figure1.png}
\caption{Overview of MRD-Wood (Mapping Representation Drift). Frozen pretrained backbones are evaluated under synthetic and real acquisition shifts. Representation drift quantifies the lab-to-field gap and localizes part of it in the model itself, which manifests as two WoodID-specific boundaries---real cross-source collapse and cross-magnification asymmetry---and supports a derived label-free detection signal.}
\label{fig:mrd_framework}
\end{figure}

Formally, let $D_0=\{(x_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^{N}$ denote a clean image set and let $T_\delta$ denote an acquisition-shift operator with severity $\delta$. A shifted image is written as $x_i^\delta=T_\delta(x_i)$, and a frozen pretrained backbone maps an image to a global feature vector
\begin{equation}
  z_i = f_\theta(x_i), \qquad \hat{z}_i = \frac{z_i}{\|z_i\|_2}.
\end{equation}
Normalizing to the unit sphere makes all subsequent geometry depend on direction rather than magnitude, matching the cosine decision rule. The normalized features extracted from $D_0$ also form the clean reference bank $B_0$ used in the derived mismatch-alarm evaluation.
The decision-level degradation is measured by the accuracy drop
\begin{equation}
  \Delta A_\delta = A_0 - A_\delta,
\end{equation}
where $A_0$ and $A_\delta$ are the clean and shifted accuracies.

\subsection{Paired and distributional drift}
\label{sec:global_drift}

The controlled diagnostic asks whether a perturbed image still maps close to its
own clean location in the feature space used by the decision rule.
For controlled perturbation experiments, where a clean image and its shifted
counterpart are both available, paired feature drift is defined as the cosine
displacement between the two embeddings:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_f(x_i,\delta)
  = 1 -
  \frac{f_\theta(x_i)^\top f_\theta(T_\delta(x_i))}
  {\|f_\theta(x_i)\|_2\,\|f_\theta(T_\delta(x_i))\|_2}.
\end{equation}
A value of $0$ means the perturbation did not move the representation at all,
while larger values mean the shifted feature points in an increasingly different
direction. This quantity is an \emph{oracle} diagnostic because it requires a
clean--shifted image pair, which is unavailable at deployment; it is used here
only to test whether representation displacement is empirically linked to
recognition failure.

For real shifts without same-image pairs, the paired quantity above is not
available and should not be conflated with an unpaired distributional signal. We therefore
use distributional drift between feature sets. For a source batch $S$ and target
batch $T$, the class-centroid version used in the real cross-source and
cross-magnification analyses is
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_{\mathrm{dist}}(S,T)
  =1-\frac{\bar z_S^\top \bar z_T}{\|\bar z_S\|_2\,\|\bar z_T\|_2},
  \qquad
  \bar z_S=\frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{x_i\in S} \hat z_i .
\end{equation}
This distributional signal asks whether two acquisition sources or magnification
levels occupy different regions of the frozen feature space. Tier-A and Tier-B
therefore support claims about paired feature drift $\Delta_f$, whereas Tier-C,
Tier-D, and the reference-bank monitor support claims about distributional
feature drift and degradation without same-image pairing.

Beyond the movement of individual samples, we also characterize how the overall
class structure deforms. For each class $c$, the class centroid summarizes the
typical clean location of that class on the unit sphere,
\begin{equation}
  \mu_c=\frac{1}{|D_c|}\sum_{x_i\in D_c}\hat{z}_i ,
\end{equation}
and the within- and between-class structure is summarized by intra-class
compactness and inter-class separation,
\begin{equation}
  d_{\mathrm{intra}}=
  \frac{1}{C}\sum_{c=1}^{C}\frac{1}{|D_c|}
  \sum_{x_i\in D_c}\|\hat{z}_i-\mu_c\|_2 ,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  d_{\mathrm{inter}}=
  \frac{2}{C(C-1)}\sum_{i<j}\|\mu_i-\mu_j\|_2 .
\end{equation}
Intuitively, $d_{\mathrm{intra}}$ is the average spread of samples around their
own class centre, and $d_{\mathrm{inter}}$ is the average distance between class
centres; a smaller spread and a larger centroid separation make the
nearest-neighbour decision more stable. We combine the two into a single
separability summary, the feature-geometry collapse score,
\begin{equation}
  FGCS=\frac{d_{\mathrm{inter}}}{d_{\mathrm{intra}}+\epsilon},
\end{equation}
where a higher $FGCS$ indicates a more separable feature configuration.
We also report a feature-space separability ratio ($FSR$), implemented as the
inter-class distance divided by intra-class variance; $FSR$ collapse denotes a
decrease in this ratio under shift.

Because the absolute value of $FGCS$ depends on the dataset and backbone, we
report how class geometry \emph{changes} under shift rather than its level. In
particular, inter-class collapse is the reduction in mean pairwise centroid
separation,
\begin{equation}
  \Delta d_{\mathrm{inter}}
  = d_{\mathrm{inter}}^{\mathrm{clean}} - d_{\mathrm{inter}}^{\mathrm{shift}},
\end{equation}
so a \emph{positive} value means the average distance between class centroids
\emph{decreases} under shift, shrinking inter-class margins and making confusion
more likely. This quantity measures relative contraction of the centroid
configuration rather than literal merging of all classes into a single cluster.

\paragraph{Secondary spatial diagnostics.}
Spatial clustering and CAM-proxy redistribution describe how internal
organization changes, but they are not primary predictors. We cluster normalized
spatial tokens with KMeans ($K=3$), measure granularity and permutation-matched
cluster stability, and summarize activation redistribution with a
centroid-similarity CAM proxy. Their full definitions, ablations, and qualitative
panels are provided in Supplementary Section~\ref{app:spatial_cam}.

\subsection{Derived reference-bank mismatch alarm}
\label{sec:refbank}

The paired diagnostic is unavailable for an incoming batch because no clean
version of the same sample is observed. The derived alarm therefore replaces
paired comparison with a comparison against a fixed \emph{clean reference bank}
$B_0=\{\hat{z}_i\}_{i=1}^{N}$, against which an incoming batch $B_t$ is scored.
These standard distance and two-sample statistics answer one restricted
question---how far has the incoming batch moved from $B_0$?---and are evaluated
as acquisition-mismatch alarms rather than as calibrated estimates of accuracy
loss.

The simplest monitor measures distance to the nearest clean neighbours
(point level). For an incoming image $u$, the per-sample $k$NN distance is
\begin{equation}
  s_{kNN}(u)=
  \frac{1}{k}\sum_{j\in NN_k(f_\theta(u),B_0)}
  d_{\cos}(f_\theta(u),z_j),
\end{equation}
and the monitor reports the batch mean
$S_{kNN}(B_t)=|B_t|^{-1}\sum_{u\in B_t}s_{kNN}(u)$. A coarser monitor compares
batch centroids (first-moment level),
\begin{equation}
  S_{\mathrm{centroid}}(B_t)=1-\bar{z}_0^{\top}\bar{z}_t,
  \qquad
  \bar{z}_t=\frac{\sum_{y\in B_t}\hat{z}_y}{\|\sum_{y\in B_t}\hat{z}_y\|_2},
\end{equation}
where $\bar z_0$ is the clean reference-bank centroid. A covariance-aware monitor
rescales the distance to the reference mean by the clean feature covariance
(second-moment level), using a Ledoit--Wolf estimator \citep{ledoit2004well} for
$\Sigma_0$,
\begin{equation}
  S_M(B_t)=\frac{1}{|B_t|}\sum_{y\in B_t}
  (\hat{z}_y-\mu_0)^{\top}\Sigma_0^{-1}(\hat{z}_y-\mu_0).
\end{equation}
Finally, the most distributional monitor compares the whole incoming batch to the
reference bank with a kernel two-sample statistic, the RBF maximum mean
discrepancy between $B_0=\{a_i\}_{i=1}^{n}$ and $B_t=\{b_j\}_{j=1}^{m}$,
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{MMD}^2(B_0,B_t)=
  \frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{i,j} k(a_i,a_j)
  +\frac{1}{m^2}\sum_{i,j} k(b_i,b_j)
  -\frac{2}{nm}\sum_{i,j} k(a_i,b_j),
\end{equation}
where $k(a,b)=\exp(-\gamma\|a-b\|_2^2)$ and $\gamma$ is set by a median-distance
heuristic on the compared feature batches; $\widehat{MMD}^2$ is small when the two
feature distributions coincide and grows as they diverge. All four monitors
require only the clean reference bank $B_0$ and use \emph{no labels} for the
incoming batch, which is what makes them usable without target labels.
Because a comparison-specific median heuristic changes the numerical scale of
MMD across batches, we separately repeat the 91 clean and real-shift
source--target comparisons without feature capping, under a backbone-specific
shared bandwidth estimated only from the three Tier-A clean reference banks.
Each full-feature comparison is evaluated using the comparison-specific median,
the shared median, and $0.5\times$ and $2\times$ the shared bandwidth.
MMD is computed before aggregation, so features from different datasets or
transfer directions are never mixed into one two-sample comparison.
For interpreting the real-shift result, it is useful to expose the three terms:
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{MMD}^2=K_{AA}+K_{BB}-2K_{AB},
  \quad
  K_{UV}=\frac{1}{|U||V|}\sum_{u\in U,v\in V}k(u,v).
  \label{eq:mmd_terms}
\end{equation}
When two distributions are well separated, $K_{AB}$ is small. Greater
within-batch dispersion then lowers the shifted self-similarity $K_{BB}$ and can
reduce raw MMD even when recognition failure is severe.

\subsection{Analytical account: why representation drift predicts failure}
\label{sec:analytical}

Paired drift and recognition error are coupled through decision geometry, but
they are not the same quantity. For unit-normalized features,
$\|\hat z'-\hat z\|_2=\sqrt{2\Delta_f}$, so cosine drift is exactly a spherical
displacement. Consider the cosine nearest-centroid rule with normalized class
prototypes $p_c$. For a correctly classified clean sample, define
\begin{equation}
  \Gamma(x)=\langle \hat z,p_y\rangle-\max_{c\neq y}\langle \hat z,p_c\rangle>0 .
  \label{eq:margin}
\end{equation}
Because the margin is $2$-Lipschitz, the shifted prediction is guaranteed to be
preserved when
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_f(x) < \frac{\Gamma(x)^2}{8},
  \label{eq:preserve}
\end{equation}
and therefore a previously correct sample can fail only if
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_f(x)\ \ge\ \frac{\Gamma(x)^2}{8}.
  \label{eq:necessary}
\end{equation}
This gate explains both the association and its limits. A large displacement can
remain harmless in a wide-margin region, whereas a small displacement can cause
failure near a boundary. Equation~\eqref{eq:necessary} formalizes the
distinction between continuous drift and discrete error: failure requires
crossing a sample-specific margin. Analogous local-margin statements hold for
linear and kNN rules. All observed prototype flips satisfy the necessary
condition, while its conservative safe region certifies only about 0.3\% of
records. Adding the direction of motion
improves sample-level flip ROC-AUC by only 0.005 (0.847 versus 0.842), so the margin account is
mechanistic rather than strongly predictive. Proofs, rule extensions,
margin figures, and competitor-switching results are reported in Supplementary
Section~\ref{app:margin_proofs}.

\section{Experimental Design}

The experiments are designed around a synthetic--real shift axis. Tier-A applies
synthetic perturbations to controlled datasets so that clean--shifted image pairs
are available and representation changes can be measured precisely. Tier-B
applies the same perturbation suite to additional public acquisition sources,
testing dataset generality under controlled shifts. Tier-C evaluates real
cross-source transfer on shared species, where no same-image pairing exists.
Tier-D separately isolates real optical scale shift on VN26, because
magnification changes alter the scale of anatomical patterns. Finally, a derived
reference-bank experiment asks whether acquisition mismatch can be detected
without target labels.

\subsection{Dataset tiers and their roles}

Table~\ref{tab:datasets} summarizes the dataset tiers. Tier-A datasets (WRD25,
DTSR14, PCA11) provide the main controlled perturbation setting. Tier-B datasets
(BFS46, FSDM41, GOIMAI, WOODAUTH, BD11) test whether the same drift--drop

\subsection{Backbone and perturbation selection}

We evaluate seven frozen pretrained backbones chosen to cover complementary architectural biases: a standard residual CNN (ResNet-50 \citep{he2016resnet}), an efficient scaled CNN (EfficientNet-B3 \citep{tan2019efficientnet}), a modern ConvNet (ConvNeXt-T \citep{liu2022convnext}), a hierarchical transformer (Swin-T \citep{liu2021swin}), a self-supervised foundation-style visual representation (DINOv2-B \citep{oquab2023dinov2}), a high-resolution representation network (HRNet-32 \citep{sun2019hrnet}), and a mobile architecture (MobileNetV3 \citep{howard2019mobilenetv3}). They are intentionally kept frozen. This isolates the robustness of pretrained representations and avoids conflating the analysis with supervised adaptation or fine-tuning choices.

The perturbation families are selected to approximate common acquisition failures in macroscopic WoodID. Owens-style shifts such as blur, resize, channel shift, and scratches are included because they represent known field risks, while additional stressors cover focus/optical degradation, digital compression and pixelation, sensor noise, photometric variation, rotation, and compound field-like degradation. ImageNet-C motivates the general principle of testing diverse corruptions, but this suite is not intended as a one-to-one ImageNet-C reproduction. The goal is not to claim that these perturbations exhaust all field conditions, but to create controlled, reproducible stressors that can be linked to representation-level changes.

Table~\ref{tab:severity} gives the exact severity values used to generate the cached features. Perturbations are applied to denormalized RGB tensors after resizing to the model input size and before ImageNet renormalization. Resize perturbation is implemented as a center zoom followed by bilinear resizing back to the input size; defocus blur uses a disk-shaped point-spread kernel; JPEG uses encode/decode simulation; color and illumination changes are clipped to the valid RGB range; and rotation uses the standard torchvision rotation operator. The compound presets combine Gaussian blur, brightness scaling, and JPEG compression. These values define the experimental stress test and should be interpreted as controlled acquisition proxies rather than a complete model of all field degradations.

\subsection{Frozen-feature recognition protocol}

All reported accuracies are computed on frozen feature extractors rather than on fine-tuned WoodID classifiers. For each backbone, the classifier head is removed, global pooled features are L2-normalized, and a cosine 5-nearest-neighbor decision rule is used. Clean accuracy is estimated with five stratified shuffle splits using an 80/20 train/test split within each dataset. The split is stratified at the image-file level because specimen-level metadata are not consistently available across all public datasets; therefore, clean accuracy is interpreted as a representation-separability benchmark rather than as a specimen-disjoint deployment estimate. Perturbation accuracy uses a paired gallery--query stability protocol: clean features form the labeled gallery and the corresponding perturbed features form the query set. This protocol deliberately asks whether a shifted image remains compatible with the clean feature distribution of the same acquisition set; it should therefore be interpreted as a representation-stability benchmark rather than as field generalization across specimens. Because clean features form the gallery and the perturbed features of the same images form the query in the same embedding space, accuracy drop and feature drift share part of their mechanism; Section~\ref{sec:results_drift} therefore separates the relationship from this coupling using a frozen linear probe and the margin account of Section~\ref{sec:analytical}. To reduce dependence on one decision rule, Appendix~\ref{app:additional_checks} reports kNN sensitivity, nearest-centroid classification, and a frozen linear-probe check.

The same distinction is used in external and monitoring experiments. Tier-B
experiments reuse the paired frozen-feature kNN protocol under the same synthetic
perturbations. Tier-C and Tier-D use clean features from two real acquisition
sources or magnification levels: the gallery is one source, the query is the
other, and the label space is restricted to shared accepted species names.
Because no same-image pairs exist in these real-shift tiers, their drift signal
is distributional class-centroid drift rather than paired $\Delta_f$. In the
monitoring experiment, labels are used only to define retrospective failure
labels for evaluation; the deployed monitor scores incoming features against a
clean reference bank without using target labels. The main results use $k=5$
following a conservative local-voting protocol; Appendix~\ref{app:additional_checks}
verifies that the drift/drop conclusion is stable for $k\in\{1,3,5,10\}$ and for
nearest-centroid classification.

Quantitative experiments use the standard cached-feature protocol. Images are resized to the input resolution specified by each pretrained backbone configuration (EfficientNet-B3 uses 300$\times$300; the other backbones use 224$\times$224) and normalized with ImageNet statistics. Global features are extracted from timm pretrained models with the classifier removed and L2-normalized after pooling. Spatial features use timm \texttt{features\_only} with \texttt{out\_indices=(2,)} for CNN/HRNet/Swin backbones, while DINOv2-B uses intermediate ViT tokens from block 6. Spatial clustering uses $K=3$, L2-normalized spatial tokens, KMeans with ten initializations, and no PCA. This choice keeps the spatial metrics consistent with the features used in the accuracy and drift analyses. Cluster labels are brightness-ordered only for visualization. Quantitative cluster comparison uses Hungarian matching to reduce permutation ambiguity. SGI is interpreted only as a relative comparison at fixed $K$ because the $K$ ablation in Appendix~\ref{app:spatial_cam} shows that SGI rises monotonically as the number of clusters increases. The CAM-proxy analysis uses the centroid activation map defined in Supplementary Section~\ref{app:spatial_cam}. High-resolution VN26 figures are used only for qualitative visualization of spatial clusters and CAM-proxy overlays; they are not used as the main quantitative protocol. The robustness protocol uses five stratified shuffle splits and the global random seed is 42. The implementation requires PyTorch, torchvision, timm, scikit-learn, scipy, statsmodels-compatible mixed-effects tooling, pandas, NumPy, OpenCV, Pillow, and scikit-image; feature caches were generated on GPU-enabled hardware using the same seed and library stack for the full run.

We use bootstrap confidence intervals \citep{efron1979bootstrap}, Wilcoxon/Nemenyi ranking following common classifier-comparison practice \citep{demsar2006statistical}, Kendall's $W$, Pearson and Spearman correlations, partial correlations, hierarchical regression, and \texttt{statsmodels} mixed-effects models \citep{laird1982random,seabold2010statsmodels}. These statistics serve distinct roles. Pearson $r$ measures the strength of a linear association and Spearman $\rho$ a monotonic (rank) association; reporting both guards against a result driven by a few outliers or by nonlinearity. Partial correlation reports $r$ after residualizing shared factors, with the main robustness check adding severity to dataset, backbone, and perturbation-family controls. $R^2$ is the fraction of accuracy-drop variance explained by a regression model, and $\Delta R^2$ is the incremental variance a block adds. The Friedman test ($\chi^2$) checks whether backbones have systematically different robustness ranks across conditions; Kendall's $W$ reports how concordant those ranks are ($0$ = no agreement, $1$ = identical rankings). The Nemenyi critical-difference (CD) analysis identifies which mean ranks differ significantly: backbones connected by a bar are \emph{not} significantly different. Maximum mean discrepancy (MMD) is a kernel two-sample statistic that is large when two feature distributions differ. Mixed-effects coefficients ($\beta$) estimate the drift effect while modelling grouped (non-independent) records. The Nemenyi critical-difference analysis controls family-wise comparison for the backbone-ranking post-hoc test; other reported $p$-values are interpreted alongside effect sizes, confidence intervals, and grouped robustness checks rather than as a single decision threshold. Because backbone--dataset--perturbation records are grouped rather than fully independent, the main correlation is also checked by backbone, dataset, perturbation family, perturbation severity, leave-one-dataset-out validation, mixed-effects controls, and clustered bootstrap over dataset--backbone--perturbation groups. Detector performance is evaluated with ROC-AUC, average precision, and best F1, with additional batch-size, reference-bank-size, leave-one-dataset-out, and leave-one-perturbation-family-out checks.

\section{Results and Experimental Analysis}

The analysis follows the three research questions. Tier-A establishes the
controlled association and separates what survives metadata, severity, and
same-space controls; Tier-B tests controlled generalization to additional public
sources. Tier-C and Tier-D then expose two unpaired real-shift boundaries:
cross-source acquisition pipelines and optical magnification. The final
reference-bank experiment asks whether standard distributional statistics can
detect acquisition mismatch without claiming a new monitor or a universal
severity scale. Clean accuracy is image-file stratified; for patch-derived
datasets such as PCA11 and DTSR14, train/test patches may originate from the same
physical specimen because specimen-level identifiers are not consistently
available. Absolute accuracy and $\Delta A$ are therefore
representation-separability benchmarks rather than specimen-disjoint field
estimates. Spatial and CAM-proxy analyses remain secondary diagnostics.

\subsection{The gap exists: clean accuracy does not determine robustness}
\label{sec:perturbation_results}

Figures~\ref{fig:acc_heatmap} and~\ref{fig:severity} show that clean accuracy is high for all models (0.931--0.956), but perturbation robustness differs substantially. DINOv2-B and EfficientNet-B3 have the highest mean perturbed accuracy (0.736 and 0.708) even though HRNet-32 has the highest clean accuracy (0.956), while ResNet-50 has the largest mean drop (0.297). \subsection{The gap is measurable: drift tracks degradation}
\label{sec:results_drift}

The robustness experiment shows where models fail; the feature-geometry experiment tests which representation-level changes track that failure. For each clean-shifted pair, we measure how far the shifted embedding moves from its clean counterpart and how the class geometry changes. If feature drift is only a trivial consequence of image perturbation, it should not strongly track the magnitude of accuracy degradation across different datasets, backbones, and perturbations. Conversely, if representation displacement is empirically linked to failure, larger drift should consistently correspond to larger accuracy drop.

Figure~\ref{fig:feature_geometry} and Table~\ref{tab:geometry_corr} report both
pooled and controlled associations, with the controlled estimates providing the
primary evidence. After controlling for dataset, backbone, perturbation family,
and severity, feature drift retains a positive partial correlation with accuracy
drop ($r=0.623$, $n=1596$), and in the complete-case hierarchical model it adds
$\Delta R^2=0.031$ beyond the same metadata and severity controls
(Table~\ref{tab:hier_strict}). The pooled association is larger ($r=0.908$,
Spearman $\rho=0.937$, $n=1596$; clustered-bootstrap 95\% CI for $r$:
$[0.892,0.922]$) and serves as an upper-bound diagnostic association because it
is measured in the same frozen feature space used by the cosine-kNN decision
rule. Inter-class collapse is also strongly related to degradation ($r=0.713$),
indicating that shifted features reduce class separability. FGCS change has a
weaker but still positive association ($r=0.340$), suggesting that the ratio
between inter-class separation and intra-class compactness captures part of the
failure but is less direct than paired feature displacement. Here $n=1596$
denotes aggregate backbone--dataset--perturbation records (228 non-clean Tier-A
conditions across seven backbones), whereas the margin analysis below operates
at the sample level over 9,551,148 clean-correct sample--perturbation records.
The controlled increment is modest, but the controls are experimental metadata
rather than signals available for an incoming unlabeled batch; drift is the
operationally measurable quantity at test time.

Rank-based and grouped checks complement the pooled Pearson coefficient. Feature drift has Spearman $\rho=0.937$ over the same 1596 records. After controlling for dataset, backbone, and perturbation family by residualization, the partial correlation between feature drift and accuracy drop remains high ($r=0.795$); after adding perturbation severity to the controls, the association is smaller but still positive ($r=0.623$, $n=1596$). This shows that drift partly acts as a fine-grained severity proxy, but it retains information beyond nominal severity. The relationship is also consistent within every Tier-A dataset and every backbone, as summarized in Table~\ref{tab:consistency}; these are condition-level mean-drift versus mean-drop correlations, so they illustrate the shared severity trend rather than replacing the controlled partial-correlation evidence. Leave-one-dataset-out validation remains strong: when each Tier-A dataset is held out in turn, the mean held-out feature-drift/drop correlation is $r=0.913$, with a minimum of $0.895$.

The same conclusion holds when the decision rule is changed. The frozen linear
probe in Appendix~\ref{app:additional_checks} is a multinomial logistic
classifier trained on clean training-split features and evaluated on perturbed
test-split features; it therefore never matches a query to its own clean
embedding. Under this non-kNN decision rule, feature drift still tracks accuracy
drop strongly ($r=0.922$, $\rho=0.942$, $n=1596$;
Table~\ref{tab:decision_rule}), demonstrating persistence beyond cosine-kNN
self-matching.
The probe association is slightly larger than the kNN association
($0.922>0.908$). Its persistence under a decision rule without
query-to-own-clean matching isolates the result from that specific kNN coupling;
metadata, severity, and cross-space controls address the remaining same-space
dependence.

The margin check provides a mechanistic complement. Proposition~1 is never
violated by a prototype flip, but its worst-case
safe region certifies only 0.3\% of records. A direction-aware margin improves
sample-level flip ROC-AUC by only 0.005 (0.847 versus 0.842). Thus, the margin
account explains how displacement can become error, while its conservative
bound has limited predictive coverage;
full proofs, operating points, and competitor-switching diagnostics are reported
in Supplementary Section~\ref{app:margin_proofs}.

Mixed-effects models further address grouped dependence. A random-slope model by backbone estimates a positive feature-drift effect ($\beta=1.155$, 95\% CI $[0.976,1.334]$, $p<0.001$). A crossed model with dataset and backbone effects, while controlling perturbation family and severity, still assigns feature drift a positive coefficient ($\beta=0.737$, 95\% CI $[0.692,0.783]$, $p<0.001$). A clustered bootstrap over dataset--backbone--perturbation groups gives a same-space upper-bound association of $r=0.908$ with 95\% CI $[0.892,0.922]$. These checks reduce the risk that the main result is only a severity artifact, a single-dataset effect, or pseudo-replication.

Both mixed-effects analyses are linear models on accuracy drop. The random-slope
model uses feature drift as a fixed effect with backbone-specific random slopes.
The crossed model uses feature drift, perturbation family, and severity as fixed
effects, with dataset and backbone entered as crossed random-effect groups. These
models are used as grouped-dependence checks rather than as deployment predictors.

\subsection{The gap is partly in the model, not only the image}

The strongest evidence comes from the Tier-C source-vs-species probe
(Fig.~\ref{fig:source_species_probe}). On the same accepted species, a
two-centroid rule recovers acquisition source with accuracy 0.954 for
BFS46$\leftrightarrow$FSDM41 and 0.967 for
DTSR14$\leftrightarrow$WOODAUTH, while cross-source species recognition is only
0.014 and 0.278. Frozen features therefore retain a readily recoverable source
signature even when species geometry fails to align across sources. The global
between-source centroid distance is 0.699 times the within-source
between-species distance for BFS46$\leftrightarrow$FSDM41 but 1.436 times for
DTSR14$\leftrightarrow$WOODAUTH. Thus, source-dominant and misaligned species
geometry can coexist without reducing to a single global offset.

\paragraph{Tier-B controlled generalization.}

Applying the same perturbation suite to five additional public sources gives
$r=0.870$ with mean drop 0.450, compared with the Tier-A upper-bound association
$r=0.908$ and mean drop 0.257. This is a controlled dataset-generalization check,
not field validation; the full plot and per-source discussion are provided in
Supplementary Section~\ref{app:tierb}.
Taken together, these model-level checks point forward to the monitor analysis,
where the same source-sensitive organization is quantified as a
marginal-versus-class-conditional gap
(Section~\ref{sec:refbank_results}).

\subsection{Consequence 1: the real cross-source transfer boundary}
\label{sec:tierc_cross_source}

Tier-C tests the same diagnostic idea in an unpaired real-shift setting. The
label space is restricted to accepted species names shared by two public sources:
BFS46$\leftrightarrow$FSDM41 is the primary pair with 24 shared species, and
DTSR14$\leftrightarrow$WOODAUTH is a secondary pair with four shared temperate
species. For each ordered direction and backbone, clean features from one source
form the gallery and clean features from the other source form the query set. The
drift signal is the class-centroid distributional drift
$\Delta_{\mathrm{dist}}$, not paired $\Delta_f$, because no source provides
matched images of the same specimen under both acquisition protocols.

The primary BFS46$\leftrightarrow$FSDM41 transfer is almost completely
non-transferable despite the 24-species shared label space: mean accuracy is
0.008 for BFS46$\rightarrow$FSDM41 and 0.013 for FSDM41$\rightarrow$BFS46 over
the seven backbones. The secondary DTSR14$\leftrightarrow$WOODAUTH pair is less
catastrophic but still far below within-source performance, with mean accuracy
0.316 for DTSR14$\rightarrow$WOODAUTH and 0.324 for
WOODAUTH$\rightarrow$DTSR14. Directional asymmetry is small in both pairs
($-0.005$ and $-0.008$), so the dominant Tier-C effect is not directionality but
source incompatibility.
For BFS46$\leftrightarrow$FSDM41, the directional accuracies
0.008--0.013 are also below the 24-class chance level
($1/24\approx0.042$), indicating systematically misaligned cross-source
geometry rather than only weak transfer. This interpretation remains subject to
the coarseness of accepted-name matching across datasets: a shared GBIF name
does not guarantee identical taxonomic granularity, specimen provenance, or
labeling practice.
Together with the source probe, this below-chance result is the
class-conditional failure that the later marginal MMD decomposition cannot
recover from pooled feature distributions alone.

Distributional drift remains informative, but the relationship is weaker and
shifted upward relative to the paired Tier-A line. Over 392
species--direction--backbone records, class-centroid drift correlates positively
with cross-source accuracy drop ($r=0.331$). The cross-source fitted slope is
0.302, only 0.291 times the Tier-A slope, with a mean absolute residual of 0.488
on the Tier-A line. The reason is visible in Fig.~\ref{fig:tierc_cross_source}:
many records have drops near one, especially in BFS46$\leftrightarrow$FSDM41,
creating a saturation regime where additional centroid drift cannot linearly
explain much more degradation.
In this saturated regime, the $r=0.331$ coefficient is weakly identified as a
predictive effect; the substantive Tier-C result is qualitative---real
cross-source transfer is far harsher than any synthetic perturbation, and
distributional drift retains only directional, not calibrated, agreement with
degradation.

Cross-source degradation combines acquisition change with source, specimen, and
distribution differences. Accordingly, the experiment establishes a severe
cross-source transfer gap and a positive but coarse distributional-drift signal,
without assigning the full failure to acquisition change alone.

\subsection{Consequence 2: cross-magnification asymmetry}

Cross-magnification results on VN26 reveal the WoodID-specific deployment
scenario. Within-magnification accuracy is near perfect (0.996 at x10, 1.000 at
x20, and 0.995 at x50), but the full 3$\times$3 transfer matrix in
Table~\ref{tab:crossmag} shows that accuracy drops sharply whenever the training
and testing magnifications differ. Transfer from x10 to x20 remains partially
viable (0.619), but transfer involving x50 is much weaker, especially
x20$\rightarrow$x50 (0.160), x50$\rightarrow$x20 (0.179), and
x10$\rightarrow$x50 (0.202).

Magnification transfer is both asymmetric and non-monotone in scale ratio
(Fig.~\ref{fig:crossmag_asymmetry}). The adjacent x10--x20 pair
favours coarse-to-fine transfer: x10$\rightarrow$x20 exceeds
x20$\rightarrow$x10 by 0.082 (backbone-bootstrap 95\% CI
$[0.052,0.119]$), and all seven backbones agree on this sign. The
x10--x50 pair reverses direction: x50$\rightarrow$x10 exceeds
x10$\rightarrow$x50 by 0.081 (95\% CI for the signed
coarse-to-fine asymmetry $[-0.142,-0.022]$), with 85.7\% backbone sign
agreement. The x20--x50
pair is near-symmetric on average ($|A|=0.019$) and has weak sign agreement
(57.1\%), which is insufficient to establish a stable transfer direction.
Transfer is also not
monotone in the magnification ratio: the 5$\times$ x10--x50 pair has higher mean
bidirectional accuracy (0.242) than the 2.5$\times$ x20--x50 pair (0.170),
indicating that x50 is the locus of cross-scale incompatibility rather than scale
ratio alone. The associated spatial stability analysis is shown in
Appendix~\ref{app:spatial_cam}.

This pattern has a WoodID-specific interpretation. At x50, the apparent scale of
vessels, rays, parenchyma, and local texture fragments changes enough that
features learned from coarser galleries no longer describe the same anatomical
evidence. Transfer is therefore asymmetric: information visible at a coarser
scale is not simply recoverable after moving to a finer view, and fine-scale
texture statistics do not necessarily aggregate back into the same coarser
feature neighbourhood. The sign reversal between x10--x20 and x10--x50 is
consistent with this non-invertible scale change. It makes the VN26 result more
than a generic domain-shift example: the failure follows from the optical scale
at which wood anatomy is represented.
An additional, non-exclusive explanation is that x50 covers a smaller field of
view, changing spatial-frequency content and the match between anatomical
structures and the frozen backbone's effective receptive field.

The cross-magnification asymmetry analysis further tests whether this optical
scale failure is a separate phenomenon or another instance of representation
drift. Using class-centroid drift between matched species across magnifications,
cross-magnification accuracy drop correlates with drift at
$r=0.578$ over 42 directed backbone--magnification records, and the
cross-magnification regression slope is 0.809 times the Tier-A drift--drop slope.
The Tier-A drift--drop line is fit on WRD25, DTSR14, and PCA11 only; VN26 is
held out from that fit, so the Tier-D overlay is a genuine held-out comparison.
The mean absolute residual on the Tier-A line is 0.337, so the cross-magnification
points do not follow the synthetic-perturbation trend exactly. They nevertheless
show the same positive drift--failure direction while exposing a
WoodID-specific scale-transfer effect. Magnification asymmetry is therefore the
domain-specific failure mode, and representation drift is the common diagnostic
axis connecting it to the controlled perturbation benchmark.

\subsection{A derived label-free acquisition-mismatch alarm}
\label{sec:refbank_results}
\label{sec:monitoring_results}

The final experiment evaluates a deployment-oriented consequence of the
diagnosis. Paired feature drift is unavailable for an incoming batch because the
system does not observe a clean version of the same image. We therefore construct
a clean reference bank for each dataset/backbone and score shifted batches
without target labels. A record is retrospectively labelled as a recognition
failure when its kNN accuracy drop exceeds 0.20. Labels define only this
evaluation target; they do not enter the alarm score. The experiment is a
batch-level benchmark and real-shift validity check, not a fielded monitoring
system.

Table~\ref{tab:monitor} reports results over 4256
backbone--dataset--perturbation batch records, with bootstrap confidence
intervals for ROC-AUC and F1. RBF-MMD reaches batch-level ROC-AUC 0.968
(95\% CI 0.964--0.973) and F1 0.909 (95\% CI 0.902--0.918), compared with
batch-level ROC-AUC 0.976 for the paired feature-drift oracle evaluated on the
same 4,256 monitor records.
Leave-one-perturbation-family-out evaluation over held-out batch records gives
mean ROC-AUC 0.946 and mean F1 0.675 (Table~\ref{tab:lopo}). Thus, acquisition mismatch can
be detected without target labels or clean--shifted pairs in this benchmark, but
thresholds remain shift-family dependent.

Figure~\ref{fig:monitor_roc} instead compares controlled diagnostic signals over
1596 condition-level Tier-A records. In that protocol, paired feature drift
reaches condition-level ROC-AUC 0.975 and remains stable when the accuracy-drop threshold varies
from 0.10 to 0.40 (ROC-AUC range 0.963--0.984). Its value differs slightly from
the 0.976 batch-level oracle in Table~\ref{tab:monitor} because the record sets
and aggregation levels differ. The figure is therefore a diagnostic comparison,
not a reference-bank deployment result.

Sensitivity and transfer checks are reported in Supplementary
Section~\ref{app:additional_checks}. They show strong leave-one-dataset-out
transfer but weaker fixed-threshold transfer to unseen perturbation families
(mean held-out ROC-AUC 0.946 and F1 0.675), so calibration remains part of the
protocol rather than a solved deployment problem.

We also apply the same RBF-MMD reference-bank score to the real-shift tiers
(Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:monitor_real_shift}). Using the synthetic benchmark's best-F1
threshold (0.0756), the monitor produces no false alarms on 21 clean Tier-A
holdout batches. Real-shift batches are one ordered source--target/backbone
query set scored against its corresponding clean reference bank, with RBF-MMD
computed on at most 512 sampled feature vectors per side. Under this protocol,
the monitor flags 32/42 Tier-D cross-magnification batches (76.2\%) and 28/28
Tier-C cross-source batches (100\%); pooled over Tier-C and Tier-D, the
real-shift alarm count is 60/70 batches. This provides a real-shift validity
check beyond the synthetic benchmark: the detector fires on the evaluated
unpaired acquisition mismatches. Binary detection and severity estimation are
distinct evaluation targets: the calibrated capped-score protocol yields the
60/70 alarm count, whereas the analyses below assess whether raw score magnitude
tracks accuracy loss.
For cross-condition magnitude comparisons, Supplementary
Fig.~\ref{fig:monitor_real_shift}
uses the full-feature MMD decomposition scores rather than the capped
alarm-score draws. These mean RBF-MMD scores are not monotone in the
coarse intended tier ordering used in this real-shift monitor table
(Spearman $\rho=0.300$ against the predefined clean/Tier-B/Tier-D/Tier-C
ordering): the extremely low-accuracy BFS46$\leftrightarrow$FSDM41 transfer has
a lower mean MMD score (0.102) than the less severe
DTSR14$\leftrightarrow$WOODAUTH transfer (0.193).
The pairs also differ in shared label-space size (24 versus four species).
Matching BFS46$\leftrightarrow$FSDM41 to four randomly selected shared species
over 20 seeds raises its mean MMD from 0.102 to 0.209
(SD 0.041), compared with 0.193 for
DTSR14$\leftrightarrow$WOODAUTH. Thus much of the raw contrast is attributable
to shared-class composition and reference-bank geometry.
Crucially, matching the class count does not align recognition failure
(Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:matched_class_dissociation}). The two pairs then
have similar marginal MMD (0.209 versus 0.193), but their mean kNN-5 transfer
accuracies are 0.195 and 0.320, corresponding to failures of 0.805 and 0.680.
Class-count matching therefore removes the raw MMD inversion on average but
leaves a 0.125 failure gap: marginal distribution distance and
class-conditional transfer remain distinct quantities. Seed-level variability
and chance comparisons are reported in the Supplementary Material.
The real-shift result therefore characterizes RBF-MMD as a high-recall
label-free acquisition-mismatch alarm whose raw score does not provide a
universal severity scale across heterogeneous source pairs. Agreement with the
predefined severity ordering is $\rho=0.300$, whereas agreement with measured
failure is
$\rho=0.548$ over eight condition groups and $\rho=0.486$ over the coarser
six-group aggregation below.

The severity-dissociation check in Supplementary
Fig.~\ref{fig:monitor_severity_dissociation}
separates shift detection from severity ranking more explicitly. Averaged by
condition, every non-clean condition has a higher mean RBF-MMD score than the
clean Tier-A baseline, so detecting that a batch has moved away from $B_0$ is
straightforward in this benchmark. Ranking how damaging the shift is remains
harder: using the true measured failure rates for the eight condition groups in
this analysis, the condition-level Spearman correlation between mean MMD and
failure is only $\rho=0.548$. The most severe real shift,
BFS46$\leftrightarrow$FSDM41, has mean failure 0.990 but a lower uncapped
full-feature MMD
(0.102) than the less severe DTSR14$\leftrightarrow$WOODAUTH transfer
(failure 0.680, MMD 0.193). A secondary spread regression improves descriptive
fit across eight heterogeneous groups but is not stable within Tier-C
(standardized $\beta=-0.050$); full results are reported in Supplementary
Fig.~\ref{fig:monitor_severity_dissociation}. These results characterize
RBF-MMD as a label-free acquisition-mismatch statistic that supports binary
alarming rather than as a scalar estimate of accuracy loss.

The bandwidth-control experiment shows that the same raw ordering is not
specific to the comparison-specific median heuristic
(Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:mmd_gamma}): shared bandwidths change the
absolute scale but do not repair the condition-level severity ordering. The
kernel decomposition explains why this
limitation is structural rather than solely a bandwidth effect. Because MMD is
symmetric, we treat each source pair as an undirected comparison and report the
pooled cross-source similarity $K_{AB}$; reversing source and target gives the
same MMD. The cross term $-2\Delta K_{AB}$ contributes 0.035 (38.8\%) of the
0.091 between-pair gap,
while the combined self-term change contributes 0.056. The nearly catastrophic
BFS46$\leftrightarrow$FSDM41 transfer has \emph{higher} pooled cross-source
similarity ($K_{AB}=0.5875$ versus 0.5698). MMD compares marginal feature
distributions and cannot determine whether species-conditional components
remain aligned: pooled overlap can remain high when one species is displaced
toward another species' feature region. This interpretation is consistent with
the source-dominant representation found by the source-versus-species probe
(Fig.~\ref{fig:source_species_probe}) and the near-zero cross-source transfer, while
the shared-class control confirms that reference-bank composition also changes
the marginal score (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:mmd_confound}). Thus the
monitor can detect acquisition mismatch without ranking the resulting
class-conditional failure, directly motivating species-conditional source
alignment rather than unconditional marginal alignment.

The practical implication is that feature drift should be viewed in two forms.
The paired version is a controlled upper-bound diagnostic for the
representation-failure mechanism. The reference-bank version is a
deployment-oriented mismatch alarm: it compares incoming features to the clean
reference bank $B_0$ and can trigger inspection, reacquisition, or recalibration.
As a benchmark-specific starting recipe, the RBF-MMD alarm uses batches of at least
32--64 images where possible, a clean reference bank of several hundred images
per source/backbone, and the synthetic best-F1 threshold 0.0756; this operating
point should be recalibrated for the target acquisition protocol and
false-alarm tolerance. The small gap between RBF-MMD and the oracle paired drift
supports its use as a screening signal in the evaluated setting. Under real
shift, the synthetic-calibrated threshold flags severe unpaired acquisition
mismatch, whereas raw monitor scores do not provide a universally calibrated
severity scale. An operational alarm
threshold should therefore be calibrated for the expected batch size,
reference-bank coverage, acquisition condition, and tolerated false-alarm rate.

Two supplementary checks examine mitigation separately from detection. Naive
Wiener deblurring worsens both accuracy and drift, while a
single-cell augmentation probe reduces but does not eliminate the known-shift
gap. Neither establishes species-conditional source invariance under the real
shifts.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{What the diagnosis establishes}

The controlled results support a bounded conclusion. Representation drift
contains a modest signal beyond dataset, backbone, perturbation family, and
severity (partial $r=0.623$, $\Delta R^2=0.031$), while only a small component
transfers across feature spaces ($r=0.178$). The pooled same-space correlation
($r=0.908$) is therefore an upper bound, not the principal evidence. This pattern
is scientifically informative: drift is largely a fine-grained severity proxy
plus a representation-specific component, with a smaller transferable core.
The source-vs-species probe strengthens that reading by showing that frozen
features retain acquisition-source identity even when species geometry does not
align across sources. Together with the margin gate, these results explain why
drift can track errors without making the relationship definitional or causal.

The real-shift analyses reveal a single class-conditional thread across three
otherwise different tests. The source-versus-species probe
(Fig.~\ref{fig:source_species_probe}) shows that acquisition source remains
recoverable; the below-chance cross-source result
(Section~\ref{sec:tierc_cross_source}) shows that species identity is
systematically misaligned across those sources; and the kernel decomposition
(Section~\ref{sec:refbank_results}) shows why a marginal monitor cannot fully
represent that failure. Together, these observations identify the evaluated
lab-to-field gap as class-conditional misalignment within a source-dominated
marginal organization. This mechanism explains both cross-source collapse and
the monitor's detect-but-not-rank behaviour, and motivates
species-conditional source invariance as the mitigation target.

\subsection{WoodID-specific consequences}

The two real-shift experiments expose consequences that clean accuracy and
generic corruption tests miss. First, shared-species cross-source transfer can
collapse even when source identity remains easily recoverable, indicating that
the frozen representation organizes acquisition source more consistently than
cross-source species identity. Second, magnification transfer is asymmetric and
non-monotone: x50 is a locus of representational failure, and the easier transfer
direction changes across magnification pairs. This is not adequately described
as generic image corruption. Optical scale changes which vessels, rays,
parenchyma, and texture fragments are represented and can remove information
needed to invert the transfer. These findings also explain why model selection
must be acquisition-specific: a backbone that separates one clean source well
need not preserve species geometry across devices, sources, or magnifications.

\subsection{Detection is not mitigation}

Reference-bank monitoring is a practical consequence of the diagnosis, not the
paper's methodological novelty. Standard MMD and distance scores detect many
risky batches without target labels, but their raw magnitude is not a calibrated
accuracy-loss scale. The raw cross-source inversion persists under shared
kernel bandwidths but disappears on average after matching shared-class count,
showing that cross-dataset MMD magnitude depends on reference-bank composition.
More fundamentally, the kernel cross term measures pooled source overlap rather
than preservation of species-conditional geometry: the catastrophic
BFS46$\leftrightarrow$FSDM41 pair has greater $K_{AB}$ than the milder pair.
This marginal-versus-conditional mismatch links the monitor limitation to the
source-dominant geometry diagnosed above. The matched four-species control
isolates the same limitation from label-space size: MMD differs by only 0.016,
whereas failure remains 0.125 higher for BFS46/FSDM41.
A fixed threshold transferred to unseen perturbation
families with F1 falling from 0.909 in the pooled benchmark to 0.675 under
leave-one-family-out evaluation; near-1\% failure families also make some held-out
evaluations degenerate.
Accordingly, the proposed recipe is an acquisition-mismatch alarm: use batches
of at least 32--64 images where possible, several hundred validated reference
features per source/backbone, and calibrate the threshold to the target
false-alarm tolerance. The benchmark threshold 0.0756 is a starting point, not a
universal operating value.

More importantly, detecting source-sensitive geometry does not repair it.
Known-shift augmentation can improve one evaluated cell, whereas naive
deblurring can worsen both drift and accuracy; neither establishes robustness to
unanticipated field shifts. The longer-term research direction is therefore to
learn representations that preserve species-discriminative anatomy while
suppressing acquisition-source identity---more precisely, invariance to source
conditional on species, since fully removing source information can discard
useful signal when source and species distributions are correlated. Such
species-conditional source alignment, evaluated with specimen-disjoint and
matched laboratory-to-field captures, is the natural mitigation target suggested
by the present diagnosis.

\section{Limitations}

This study has several limitations. First, synthetic perturbations approximate
but do not exhaust real deployment shifts. The suite is broader than a single
selected-shift study, but it is still a controlled diagnostic set rather than a
full ImageNet-C-style corruption benchmark or a complete model of field
acquisition. Outdoor acquisition, specimen preparation artifacts, operator
behavior, and device-specific optics may introduce shifts not covered here. We
mitigate the dataset-generalization concern with external Tier-B datasets under
the same perturbation suite and separately test real distributional shifts using
Tier-C cross-source transfer and Tier-D VN26 cross-magnification; however, paired
laboratory-to-field captures of the same specimens were not available, so direct
validation against true field shift remains future work. A matched field study
with repeated captures of the same specimens under laboratory and operational
conditions is therefore essential before fixing field operating points. Tier-C cross-source
drop also mixes acquisition change with genuine source, specimen, and
distribution differences; the corresponding conclusion is therefore scoped to
distributional drift tracking degradation under real cross-source transfer, not
to a full causal explanation of all cross-source failure. Augmentation training
is a natural mitigation hypothesis for this perturbation suite. The frozen
backbone protocol isolates intrinsic representation stability from adaptation,
and a single-case partial check in
Section~\ref{sec:augmentation} shows that augmentation training of the head
reduces but does not remove the gap on DINOv2-B/WRD25 while the drift diagnostic
persists; generalization across backbone--dataset cells, full fine-tuning,
supervised domain adaptation, and test-time adaptation remain future work.
Second, clean-split evaluation is image-file stratified because specimen-level
identifiers are not consistently available across all public datasets; therefore,
clean accuracy should not be interpreted as specimen-disjoint field performance,
especially for patch-derived datasets. Specimen leakage where present can
inflate absolute clean and shifted accuracies, but it does not by itself create
the within-record drift/drop association; the relationship is additionally
supported on non-patch Tier-B sources such as WOODAUTH, GOIMAI, and BFS46 and
under the frozen linear probe. Specimen-disjoint validation remains future work
pending public datasets with consistent specimen identifiers. Because the
evaluated public metadata do not provide a consistent specimen, session, device,
or sheet grouping across sources, the possible inflation cannot be quantified
with a source-cluster holdout in the present study. Third, the
correlation analyses use grouped backbone--dataset--perturbation records. We
report within-group, rank, partial-correlation, mixed-effects,
clustered-bootstrap, and leave-one-dataset-out checks, but these remain
observational analyses rather than causal interventions. Fourth, CAM-proxy maps
are explanatory proxies and do not establish causal attribution. Fifth, spatial
clusters are unsupervised representation clusters, not expert-labeled anatomical
tissues; anatomical validation would require pixel- or region-level expert
annotations that are not available in the evaluated datasets. Sixth, the
high-resolution VN26 panels are qualitative visualizations, whereas the main
quantitative protocol uses standard cached features for consistency across
experiments. Finally, reference-bank monitoring depends on batch size, threshold
calibration, shift family, and the representativeness of the clean reference bank
$B_0$; if the reference bank is biased or incomplete, the monitor can misestimate
deployment risk. Its raw RBF-MMD magnitude also depends on kernel bandwidth.
The shared-bandwidth control shows that the raw cross-source ordering is not
caused by the per-comparison median heuristic, while the matched-class control
shows that it is sensitive to label-space composition. Neither control
establishes universal severity calibration for unseen field shifts.

\section{Conclusion}

MRD-Wood provides a controlled diagnosis of WoodID failure under acquisition
shift. After metadata and severity controls, paired drift retains partial
$r=0.623$ and adds $\Delta R^2=0.031$; the smaller cross-space partial
correlation ($r=0.178$) shows that the transferable diagnostic core is real but
limited. The pooled $r=0.908$ is consequently an upper bound shaped by severity
and same-space coupling. Source identity is highly recoverable from frozen
features even when cross-source species recognition fails, locating part of the
laboratory-to-field gap in the representation rather than only in image quality.

The principal domain findings are the cross-source collapse boundary and the
asymmetric, non-monotone VN26 magnification transfer. A standard reference-bank
monitor can flag many shifted batches without target labels, but it requires
shift-specific calibration and is not a universal severity meter: even after
matching both Tier-C pairs to four species, similar marginal MMD coexists with
materially different class-conditional transfer failure. The study
therefore closes with a mitigation question rather than a monitoring claim:
future WoodID systems should explicitly align species geometry across
acquisition sources and optical scales while suppressing source identity
conditional on species, rather than removing all source information when source
and species distributions are correlated.
Establishing that capability will require specimen-disjoint, matched
laboratory-to-field validation.

\subsection{Secondary diagnostic definitions}

Let $S(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{H_sW_s\times d}$ be the spatial token map extracted from
a backbone. We $L_2$-normalize the tokens and apply KMeans \citep{lloyd1982least}
with $K=3$ and without PCA in the final quantitative protocol. We fix $K=3$ as a
low-complexity diagnostic choice rather than an anatomical claim: the raw-token
grid $K$ ablation shows that average SGI increases monotonically as $K$ grows,
while average silhouette generally decreases. KMeans seed sensitivity is small:
the SGI coefficient of variation remains below 0.3\% for EfficientNet-B3 and
ConvNeXt-T, below 1.4\% for ResNet-50, HRNet-32, and Swin-T, and below 3.6\% for
DINOv2-B and MobileNetV3. The cluster labels are represented on a common
image-aligned grid $L(x)\in\{1,\ldots,K\}^{H_c\times W_c}$ for connected-component
and overlap computations. For visualization only, clusters are brightness-ordered
as $C_1,C_2,C_3$.

The spatial granularity index measures how fragmented the clustered map is,
\begin{equation}
  SGI=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K} n_{\mathrm{comp}}(C_k)}{H_cW_c},
\end{equation}
where $n_{\mathrm{comp}}(C_k)$ is the number of connected components in cluster
$k$; a higher $SGI$ means the backbone partitions the surface into more
disconnected pieces. To measure whether the same regions reappear after the image
is perturbed, we compute the cluster stability between a clean image and its
shifted counterpart as the mean per-cluster overlap,
\begin{equation}
  CSI=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}
  \mathrm{IoU}(C_k^{\mathrm{clean}}, C_k^{\mathrm{shift}}).
\end{equation}
Because cluster identities are arbitrary and may permute between the clean and
shifted runs, we also report a permutation-invariant variant that matches clean
and shifted clusters optimally via the Hungarian algorithm
\citep{kuhn1955hungarian},
\begin{equation}
  CSI_H=\frac{1}{K}\max_{\pi}
  \sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathrm{IoU}(C_k^{\mathrm{clean}}, C_{\pi(k)}^{\mathrm{shift}}),
\end{equation}
so that a stable region is rewarded even if its cluster label changes.

For activation allocation, let $s_u(x)$ be the $L_2$-normalized spatial feature at
location $u$, and let $\nu_c$ be the $L_2$-normalized centroid of class $c$
computed from spatial features pooled over the same condition. For an image $x_i$
with class label $y_i$, the CAM-proxy is
\begin{equation}
  M_{y_i}(u)=
  \frac{\max(0, s_u(x_i)^\top \nu_{y_i})-\min_v \max(0, s_v(x_i)^\top \nu_{y_i})}
  {\max_v \max(0, s_v(x_i)^\top \nu_{y_i})-\min_v \max(0, s_v(x_i)^\top \nu_{y_i})+\epsilon}.
  \label{eq:cam}
\end{equation}
Intuitively, Equation~\eqref{eq:cam} assigns a high score to spatial locations
whose feature points in the same direction as the class centroid, then rescales
the scores to $[0,1]$; it is a similarity-based saliency proxy, not a
gradient-based or causal one. The resulting heatmap is bilinearly resized to the
cluster grid before aggregation.

The mass assigned to cluster $k$ is the share of total activation falling inside
that cluster,
\begin{equation}
  p_k=\frac{\sum_{u\in C_k}M(u)}{\sum_u M(u)+\epsilon},
\end{equation}
its concentration is measured by entropy,
\begin{equation}
  H(p)=-\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_k\log(p_k+\epsilon),
\end{equation}
where a low entropy means the activation is concentrated in few clusters, and the
\emph{redistribution} between a clean image and its shifted counterpart is
measured by the Jensen--Shannon divergence \citep{lin1991divergence},
\begin{equation}
  JSD(p,q)=\frac{1}{2}KL(p\|m)+\frac{1}{2}KL(q\|m),
  \qquad m=\frac{p+q}{2},
\end{equation}
which is large when the activation mass moves to different clusters under shift.
These spatial and CAM-proxy metrics are explanatory signals; they do not
establish causality.

\subsection{Proof details and margin diagnostics}
\label{app:margin_proofs}

\noindent\textbf{Lemma 1.}
For unit vectors $\hat z,\hat z'$,
$\|\hat z'-\hat z\|_2^2=\|\hat z'\|_2^2+\|\hat z\|_2^2-2\langle \hat z,\hat z'\rangle
=2-2(1-\Delta_f)=2\Delta_f$, hence
$\|\hat z'-\hat z\|_2=\sqrt{2\,\Delta_f}$.

\noindent\textbf{Lemma 2.}
Each prototype score $\hat z\mapsto\langle \hat z,p_c\rangle$ is
$1$-Lipschitz because $\|p_c\|_2=1$. A pointwise maximum of $1$-Lipschitz
functions is $1$-Lipschitz, so
$\max_{c\neq y}\langle\cdot,p_c\rangle$ is $1$-Lipschitz. The prototype margin is
the difference between two $1$-Lipschitz functions and is therefore
$2$-Lipschitz:
$|\Gamma(\hat z')-\Gamma(\hat z)|\le2\|\hat z'-\hat z\|_2$.

\noindent\textbf{Proposition 1.}
By Lemmas 1 and 2,
$\Gamma(\hat z')\ge \Gamma(\hat z)-2\|\hat z'-\hat z\|_2
=\Gamma(x)-2\sqrt{2\Delta_f}$. If
$\Delta_f<\Gamma(x)^2/8$, then
$2\sqrt{2\Delta_f}<2\sqrt{2\cdot\Gamma(x)^2/8}=\Gamma(x)$, hence
$\Gamma(\hat z')>0$ and the shifted feature keeps the correct label.

For a frozen linear probe with class weights $\{w_c\}$ and scores
$g_c(\hat z)=w_c^\top\hat z+b_c$, each score is $\|w_c\|_2$-Lipschitz. With
$L=2\max_c\|w_c\|_2$, the analogous necessary condition for a flip is
$\Delta_f(x)\ge \Gamma_{\mathrm{lin}}(x)^2/(2L^2)$, where
$\Gamma_{\mathrm{lin}}$ is the linear score margin. For the $k$NN rule, the same
logic applies with a local margin defined by the distance gap between the
nearest same-class and nearest different-class gallery points; a flip requires
drift large enough to reorder those neighbours.

At sample level, the necessary condition in Eq.~\eqref{eq:necessary} is satisfied
by all observed prototype flips. This is the expected contrapositive consistency
check, not a separate empirical discovery. The safe region is conservative,
certifying only 0.3\% of records (Fig.~\ref{fig:margin_crossing}). The exact
shifted margin against the clean nearest competitor identifies 53.3\% of flips
with zero false positives (Fig.~\ref{fig:margin_direction}), but the final winner
is that competitor in only 26.2\% of the 3,287,057 valid competitor-switching
records; severe blur, pixelation, and
compound shifts frequently reorder multiple competitors
(Fig.~\ref{fig:competitor_switching}).

\begin{table}[pos=htbp]

\paragraph{Negative-control deblurring.}
We also ran a negative-control restoration check on Gaussian-blur conditions to
test whether a naive frequency-domain Wiener intervention, using an assumed
Gaussian point-spread function and fixed noise level, reduces representation
drift. It does not in this scoped setting. Averaged over 126
backbone--dataset--blur-radius records, blurred accuracy is 0.469, while the
deblurred output drops to 0.297. Mean paired drift also increases from 0.488 to
0.638. Deblurring improves accuracy in only 10.3\% of records and reduces
representation drift in only 8.7\% of records. Table~\ref{tab:deblur}
summarizes the result by blur radius. Acquisition-quality corrections therefore
require validation against both representation stability and recognition
behavior.
